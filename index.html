The ads you can’t see

Adapted from Angus, Hayden & Carah, The Conversation (2026). Opinion / analysis.

¶1Online advertising is about to change dramatically. At its Google Marketing Live event, Google presented expanded artificial intelligence (AI) systems for digital ads. Soon, ads could appear inside your conversation with an AI chatbot, or you could be offered a discount that only you see, chosen by AI from your browsing behaviour, how likely you are to buy, and what is available nearby. Companies will also be able to use AI tools to produce ads from start to finish.

¶2Google is not alone. Meta is expanding tools that automatically generate and personalize ad images, video backgrounds, captions and targeting across Facebook and Instagram. TikTok’s owner, ByteDance, offers a suite called Symphony that can turn a simple text prompt or product link into promotional videos, scripts, AI avatars and dubbed voiceovers that look like creators’ content.

¶3At the same time, ads are becoming harder to recognize. Instagram and Facebook recently replaced their familiar “sponsored” labels with smaller “ad” markers. This “may look like a minor interface tweak,” the authors write, but it signals something larger: “the steady erosion of clear boundaries” between advertising, entertainment, recommendation and ordinary social life.

¶4Platforms have engineered ads to mimic organic content: think of influencer partnerships, AI-personalized search results, or brands posting memes. Rather than interrupting what you watch, ads are designed to “dissolve into the flow itself.” When a company buys ad space, the platform labels it automatically, but for partnerships and personalized results disclosure is limited. And, the authors point out, “the less an ad feels like an ad, the more effective it often becomes.”

¶5The authors are researchers with the Australian Ad Observatory and the Australian Internet Observatory. They have studied political advertising, “astroturfing” (fake grassroots campaigns), the marketing of alcohol and unhealthy food, and misleading “green” claims. Their work depends on simple signals: what counts as an ad, who paid for it, where it appeared and why it was shown to particular people. “But those signals are weakening.”

¶6AI makes this worse. If each person receives a private stream of product suggestions and discounts, regulators and researchers cannot audit them. Such ads could also be biased or harmful: “you might be telling an AI assistant that you’re stressed, and suddenly be offered a discount on a case of wine.” For regulated products such as alcohol and gambling, ads that appear one moment and vanish the next are almost impossible to check, so no one can be sure they comply with the rules.

¶7The authors see an opportunity in Australia’s emerging “digital duty of care” framework. Opaque ad systems, they argue, shape politics, health, money decisions and public trust. They propose minimum rules: clear ad labels that both people and machines can read; public ad archives, including AI variations, open to public-interest scrutiny; accurate information about targeting; and clear identification of AI-generated or AI-mediated ads.

¶8“This is not about banning advertising,” they stress, nor about returning to “some imagined ‘clean’ internet.” Advertising has always adapted to new media. But there is “a fundamental difference between visible persuasion and persuasion that disappears into the infrastructure.”

True, False or Not Given?

True (¶1).
False: it creates content from a text prompt or product link (¶2).
False: disclosure for partnerships is “limited” (¶4).
True (¶5).
Not Given: it is called “emerging”; we are not told whether it is in force.
False: they want labels and transparency, not a ban (¶7–8).

E. Rhetoric X-ray: how do the authors persuade?

EthosBuilds trust in the speaker: expertise, experience, fairness.
PathosMoves emotions: fear, hope, sympathy, outrage. Often a vivid example.
LogosUses facts, examples, cause and effect, logical steps.
AnticipationAnswers an objection before the reader can make it.

Which strategy does each excerpt mainly use? Some could fit two categories; choose the main one, then check and read the explanation. Discuss items 3 and 6 with a partner: is the evidence strong enough?

Pairs: in-room students sit together; online students are paired in breakout rooms. Mixed pairs (one in-room laptop + one online) also work well.

“researchers like us, working through projects such as the Australian Ad Observatory…” ¶5
Ethos: the authors present themselves as experienced experts, so we trust their judgment.
“you might be telling an AI assistant that you’re stressed, and suddenly be offered a discount on a case of wine” ¶6
Pathos: a vivid, personal hypothetical (“you”) that makes the reader feel vulnerable. Note: it is a possibility, not reported evidence.
Instagram and Facebook replaced “sponsored” labels with smaller “ad” markers. ¶3
Logos: a concrete, checkable fact. Critical question: is one label change enough to prove a “steady erosion”? The authors admit it “may look like a minor interface tweak”.
“the less an ad feels like an ad, the more effective it often becomes” ¶4
Logos: a cause-and-effect claim. It sounds logical, but no study is cited in this article. “Often” is a hedge.
“This is not about banning advertising. Nor is it about returning to some imagined ‘clean’ internet.” ¶8
Anticipation: the authors block a straw man version of their argument (“they want to ban ads!”) before critics can use it. It also builds ethos, because they sound reasonable.
Without clear signals, “we lose one of the few remaining ways to understand who is shaping the information environments we increasingly depend on every day.” conclusion
Pathos (fear of loss, urgency): “few remaining ways”, “every day”. Is this a fair warning or a mild slippery slope? Discuss.

G. The regulator’s desk: which rules first?

You are advisers to a national regulator with budget for only three new rules this year. Four come from the article; two are extra options. In groups of 3–4, put the rules in order (top 3 will be adopted). Consider impact on consumers and feasibility (can it actually be enforced?).

Groups of 3–4 with at least one online student in each group where possible. The group shares one worksheet screen (in-room laptop on the call, or an online student shares their screen). Every member re-creates the final order on their own worksheet.

  • Consistent, clear ad labels that both people and machines can read, on every format.
  • Public ad archives, including every AI-generated variation, open to researchers.
  • Accurate information about targeting: why you were shown this ad.
  • Clear identification of AI-generated or AI-mediated ads, including how AI was used.
  • (Extra) No personalized AI ads for alcohol or gambling at all.
  • (Extra) An “off switch” so users can refuse personalized prices and discounts.

H. Pushback round: platform vs. researchers

Now a tech platform pushes back against your #1 rule. Each group splits into two sides. Side A (the platform) writes two objections; Side B (the researchers) answers each one. Rule: every speaker must use one named appeal (ethos, pathos or logos), and listeners call out which one it was.

Side A and Side B can be split by location (online vs. in-room) to make turn-taking easy: the teacher alternates between the call and the room. Online listeners type the appeal they heard in the chat (“E”, “P” or “L”).

K. Fallacy toolkit

A fallacy is a flaw in reasoning that makes an argument look stronger than it is. The first three are this week’s core terms. Read the cards and add one example of your own for a core term.

Straw mancore

Distorts an opponent’s view into a weaker, extreme version, then attacks that.
Signal: “So you’re saying we should…”
“Researchers want to ban all online ads.”

Slippery slopecore

Claims one small step will lead to an extreme result, without showing each step.
Signal: “If we allow X, soon…”
“Label AI ads today, and tomorrow the government controls every post.”

Ad hominemcore

Attacks the person instead of their argument.
Signal: “Of course they would say that…”
“She only criticizes AI because she’s an old-fashioned journalist.”

Bandwagon

Something must be right because many people do or believe it.
Signal: “Everyone is already…”
“Millions of brands use AI models, so it’s fine.”

False dilemma

Presents only two options when more exist.
Signal: “Either… or…”
“Either we ban AI ads or we accept total deception.”

Hasty generalization

Draws a big conclusion from too little evidence.
Signal: “I saw one…, so all…”
“That AI ad looked fake, so AI ads are easy to spot.”

Fake fans, real sales

Adapted from Sarah Marsh, The Guardian, 21 June 2026. News investigation.

¶1A Guardian investigation found that some brands are quietly deploying AI-generated “influencers” on social media. The content purports to show genuine customers enjoying products, but gives no obvious sign that the people are not real. Some creators who make this content are asked to sign non-disclosure agreements (NDAs), so they cannot talk about their work.

¶2No specific UK rule requires brands to say when ad content was created with AI. In the EU, rules under the Artificial Intelligence Act, applying from August 2026, require AI-generated or manipulated content, such as deepfake images, audio and video, to be clearly labelled. These rules do not apply in the UK.

¶3Examples include the “crying bride” videos for the Once app: two deepfake-detection firms concluded the brand had likely used AI influencers, and Once did not respond to a request for comment. A video for Maket, an AI home-design app, shows a woman who appears to be AI-generated. Maket said AI influencers were “not a core part” of its strategy, just an experiment to test marketing ideas on a small scale. Ashle, a Dubai-based fashion brand, posted a photo of a model who appears to have an extra finger. After the Guardian contacted it, Ashle deleted photos. It said its clothes are real and handmade, that some early images used AI, and that the photos were removed because those designs were no longer sold, “not because they were AI-generated.”

¶4The consumer group Which? said customers should be clearly told when promotional content features AI-generated people. Lisa Barber of Which? said its research found that 70% of people could not correctly identify all the real and fake videos they were shown. The Advertising Standards Authority (ASA) said nothing in its rules prohibits undisclosed AI content; the question it would ask is whether the ad itself is misleading.

¶5Clarissa Mansbridge, a former celebrity manager who now creates AI influencers for brands, estimated that “about 40% to 60%” of some big brands’ content is made with AI, but said many creators are under NDA. “I call it plausible deniability,” she said. Brands want high-end images without paying $20,000–$70,000 for a photoshoot, she explained, and “human influencers killed the market for themselves.” In her view, the authenticity of user-generated content “was always about resonance, not [about] who made it,” and AI is simply “a smarter, more scalable way to get there.”

¶6Not everyone agrees. Leeds artist Zac Rossiter was offered a free AI-generated “unboxing” video of his prints by a marketing agency. He refused: “I would never work with an agency that used fake AI unboxing videos over actual, real people.”

O. Weigh the persuasion

Quoted speakers persuade too. For each statement, name the weakness, rate its strength (1 = weak, 4 = strong), and say what evidence would make it stronger.

“I’m going to say about 40% to 60% of the content out there from some of the big brands is actually being made through AI.” Mansbridge
Strength:
Vague, unsourced statistic: a 20-point range, “I’m going to say”, and no way to check (NDAs). A big number can also create a bandwagon effect that makes AI content seem normal.
“Unfortunately, human influencers killed the market for themselves.” Mansbridge
Strength:
Sweeping generalization: all human influencers are blamed as one group, which also shifts responsibility away from brands.
“The authenticity of UGC was always about resonance, not [about] who made it.” Mansbridge
Strength:
Redefining a key word: “authenticity” normally means real; she redefines it as emotionally effective, so a fake can count as “authentic”.

Strength:

P. Advertising hearing: Shoppers’ Voice v. Keepsake

The case (invented, based on the Guardian’s examples): Keepsake, a wedding photo app, posted a video of a tearful bride praising the app. The bride was AI-generated; nothing said so. The consumer group Shoppers’ Voice has complained to the regulator. The panel must decide: was the ad misleading?

The twist: each team secretly hides one fallacy in its opening statement. Opponents and the panel try to catch it. A correct catch earns a point; a false accusation loses one.

The panel works best as online students (they see and hear everyone on the call). To raise a flag: in the room, hold up a paper with the fallacy name; online, type “FLAG: straw man” in the chat. Every team must have at least one online member who speaks.

Shoppers’ Voice (complainants). Argue that viewers believed this was a real bride’s honest experience. Useful support: Which? found 70% of people could not identify all real and fake videos; the EU now requires labels for deepfakes. Anticipate: “the app features are real, so where is the harm?”
Keepsake (brand). Argue that every app feature shown is accurate, so no consumer was misled about the product. Point out that UK rules do not require AI labels. You may concede the bride was a “creative concept”. Prepare for: “Would customers feel deceived if they knew?”
AI studio (made the video). Argue that human UGC was always scripted and paid for anyway, that AI makes good marketing affordable for small brands, and that “resonance” matters more than who made the content. Prepare for: “Then why the NDA?”
Panel (3–4 students). Apply the regulator’s test from the article: is the ad misleading, and is it socially responsible? Ask each team one hard question. Track fallacy flags below and announce a ruling with two reasons.
  1. Prepare in teams: write a 90-second opening statement with ethos, pathos and logos, plus your hidden fallacy (6 min).
  2. Opening statements: complainants, brand, AI studio (5 min).
  3. Panel questions and rebuttals; flags can be raised at any time (7 min).
  4. Panel deliberates in front of everyone; teams reveal their hidden fallacies (4 min).
  5. Ruling and debrief: which argument was strongest, and why? (3 min)

Q. Letter to the regulator

Should brands be required to label AI-generated influencers? Write a 150–180-word letter to the ASA (or your own country’s advertising regulator). Your letter must:

  • state a clear position in the first two sentences;
  • use ethos, pathos and logos at least once each;
  • represent the opposing view fairly (no straw man!) before answering it;
  • cite one source with a reporting verb (e.g. Which? contends that…);
